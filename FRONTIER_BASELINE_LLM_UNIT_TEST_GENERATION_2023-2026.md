## 3. Consensus vs contested vs assumption

### 3.1 Consensus (replicated or industrially deployed, uncontested in the retrieved literature)

1. **LLMs produce tests that look human and compile more often than SBST tests, but fail on assertions.** TestPilot, ChatTester, TestGenEval, TestART independently converge: the dominant defect class is the **assertion**, not syntax. TestPilot: most failures are 2-second timeouts. ChatTester: failures are "mostly caused by incorrect assertions." TestGenEval: "frequent assertion errors when addressing complex code paths."
2. **Coverage alone is an insufficient — and partly misleading — target.** MuTAP states it explicitly ("coverage is weakly correlated with the efficiency of tests in bug detection"), and EvalPlus demonstrates the *benchmark-level* consequence: test insufficiency changes model **rankings**, not just scores.
3. **Mutation-based signals add information that coverage does not.** MuTAP (mutation score 93.57%, +28% faulty snippets), ACH (mutation-guided, deployed on 10,795 Kotlin classes), SWE-Mutation (discriminative power measured by mutants), TestGenEval (cosmic-ray mutation operators). Every 2025–2026 benchmark in this review that wants to be credible reports **mutation score alongside coverage**.
4. **Feedback loops beat one-shot prompting.** Verified as effective in: coverage feedback (CoverUp, TestART), mutation feedback (MuTAP, ACH), compile/execution error feedback (TestSpark, ChatTester, ChatUniTest), counter-example/ineffective-test feedback (TELPA).
5. **Filtering before human review is what made industrial deployment viable.** Meta's two industrial systems both wrap generation in deterministic assurance/quality gates and report **73%** engineer acceptance.
6. **Repair of generated tests is high-yield and largely mechanical.** TestART: template repair alone fixes **50%** of compile errors and **75%** of runtime errors; 40% of runtime errors are `assertEquals` expected-value mismatches, 25% are boolean-assertion inversions.

### 3.2 Contested (live disagreement with published counter-evidence)

1. **"Feedback/engineering-heavy pipelines beat plain LLM prompting" — actively contested in 2026.**
   Konstantinou, Degiovanni & Papadakis (ICST 2026) **replicated four SOTA tools** (HITS, SymPrompt, TestSpark, CoverUp) with *current* LLM versions on **393 classes / 3,657 methods** and found the **plain LLM baseline won on every effectiveness metric**: +17.72% line coverage, +19.80% branch coverage, **+20.92% mutation score**, at comparable query cost. Their diagnosis: these techniques "have been evaluated in relation to relatively weak baselines (for today's standards), i.e., old LLM versions and relatively weak prompts, which may exacerbate the performance contribution of the approaches." This is the single most consequential result in this baseline — it puts every pre-2025 comparative claim in this table under revision.
2. **Do generated tests find real faults, or do they prove nothing?** SWT-Bench found that **code-repair agents beat test-generation specialists** at producing issue-reproducing tests — i.e., the "test generation" specialization is not where the capability lives. SWE-Mutation quantifies the other direction: even the best tested model achieves **10.20% verification / 36.15% detection**, and detection collapses from **71.04% → 39.81%** when mutants are made realistic.
3. **Generalization across LLM versions vs. "the technique is the contribution."** TestPilot itself found model-dependence (gpt3.5-turbo 70.2% → StarCoder 54.0% statement coverage) yet argued effectiveness "does not fundamentally depend on the specific model." ICST 2026 directly challenges the "technique contributes" reading.
4. **Contamination vs. generalization.** TestPilot reports a genuine attempt to control for this: high coverage on **GitLab-hosted** packages (not in training data), and **no exact copies** of existing tests (92.8% ≤50% similarity). The TOSEM smell study reaches the opposite worry: overlap between LLM and human test smells "rais[es] concerns of potential data leakage from training corpora." Both are empirical; they measure different things (verbatim copying vs. stylistic overlap).
5. **Flakiness severity.** Meta's TestGen-LLM treats flakiness as a filtered nuisance (5 repeated runs; 57% pass reliably). ICSE-SEIP 2026 finds generated tests are *slightly more* flaky than existing tests and that **flakiness transfers from prompt context** — arguing developers "should prioritize fixing flaky tests before applying LLM-based generation approaches." A gate vs. a precondition.

### 3.3 Assumptions (widely used, not established by the retrieved evidence)

1. **That line/branch coverage improvements translate into fault-detection improvements.** Every generator in §1.1 reports coverage as a headline result; only MuTAP, EvalPlus, SWE-Mutation and the ICST-2026 replication measure fault detection directly. This assumption is the field's load-bearing wall.
2. **That "the generated test passes" means "the test is a correct oracle."** TestGen-LLM's own filter design concedes this: it requires passing **and** a coverage increase, precisely because passing alone is not evidence. A3Test's `assertEquals` "correction" templates (T3 replaces the expected value with the *observed actual value*) are the sharpest illustration: repair can convert a failing-but-correct assertion into a passing-but-vacuous one. **[This is my analysis of TestART's T3 template as described in the fetched text; the paper reports it as a repair win.]**
3. **That benchmark repositories are uncontaminated.** Quantified only partially: Ramos et al. (arXiv 2411.13323) find **significant memorization evidence for `codegen-multi` on Defects4J**, but "limited signs of leakage" for newer models like LLaMa 3.1. OpenAI (Feb 2026) states SWE-bench Verified "is increasingly contaminated."
4. **That single-run results are stable.** LLM APIs are nondeterministic; the flakiness study set temperature 0 *and* still repeated experiments because "LLMs are inherently non-deterministic." Most papers in §1.1 report one configuration.
5. **That "tests as documentation" is a benefit.** No retrieved source establishes a net documentation benefit; the TOSEM smell study finds generated tests systematically exhibit **Assertion Roulette** and **Magic Number Test** — readability/maintainability defects that work directly against documentation value.

## 4. The 8 metrics a rigorous 2026 test-generation paper must report

Derived from what the strongest retrieved works actually measure, plus what the critiques show is missing.

1. **Compilability/build rate** — reported *separately* from pass rate. (TestGen-LLM 75%; TestSpark's entire design goal; TestART's repair ablation 50%/75%.)
2. **Reliable pass rate under repetition** — pass rate over ≥5 executions, not one, with the flaky fraction reported. (TestGen-LLM 57% reliable across 5 runs; ICSE-SEIP 2026 executes **30×** and reports the flaky subset.)
3. **Line + branch coverage, reported jointly and never alone.** (CoverUp 80% line+branch vs CodaMosa 47%; Konstantinou reports line *and* branch *and* mutation.)
4. **Mutation score on a named, versioned tool with a stated operator set.** (MuTAP 93.57%; TestGenEval → cosmic-ray; ICST 2026 → mutation score; SWE-Mutation → mutant detection rate.)
5. **Assertion/oracle strength** — non-trivial-assertion rate, assertion-correctness rate, and coverage achieved by assertion-bearing tests only. (TestPilot 61.4% non-trivial assertions covering 61.6%; ChatTester correct-assertion deltas; TestART's runtime-error taxonomy: 40% `assertEquals`, 25% boolean assertions.)
6. **Cost**: LLM requests/tokens per generated test *and* per percentage point of coverage/mutation, plus wall-clock. (ICST 2026 uses "number of LLM queries" as the cost axis and demonstrates a **~20%** request reduction via class-first targeting.)
7. **Human acceptance / survival rate**, on a held-out or production set, with the review protocol stated. (Meta: **73%** accepted, twice.)
8. **Contamination and reproducibility controls**: benchmark provenance and recency, a live/rolling subset if available, model version + date + temperature + seeds, N repetitions with variance or confidence intervals, and a runnable artifact. (SWE-bench-Live: 1,319 tasks from post-2024 issues with per-task Docker images; SWE-bench Verified is declared "increasingly contaminated" by OpenAI, Feb 2026.)

*(Test smell count and test-suite redundancy belong in the report too, but they diagnose maintainability rather than validity — report them as a secondary quality panel: TsDetect/JNose smell prevalence, Assertion Roulette and Magic Number Test rates.)*

## 5. The 5 most common methodological flaws in this literature

1. **Weak baselines: comparing against old LLMs and weak prompts.** The ICST 2026 replication is the proof: HITS, SymPrompt, TestSpark and CoverUp all lose to a plain current-LLM prompt on line, branch **and** mutation score. Any pre-2025 headline gain in §1.1 that was measured against a 2023-era model or bare prompt is not a claim about the technique.

2. **Optimizing and reporting coverage as if it were fault detection.** MuTAP's own framing ("coverage is weakly correlated with the efficiency of tests in bug detection"), EvalPlus's ranking reversal, and SWE-Mutation's 10.20%/36.15% ceiling all show coverage is a necessary but non-sufficient statistic. Papers that report only coverage have not measured test quality.

3. **Construct invalidity of the benchmark: contamination, staleness, and measuring the wrong task.** Defects4J/HumanEval-era repositories demonstrably sit in pretraining corpora (`codegen-multi` shows significant Defects4J memorization); SWE-bench Verified is declared contaminated by OpenAI; SWT-Bench shows **code-repair agents outperform dedicated test generators**, implying "test generation" benchmarks may be measuring general agentic capability; TestEval and SWE-Mutation show that *targeted* coverage and *discriminative* detection are far harder than aggregate coverage, so aggregate-coverage benchmarks overstate capability.

4. **Single-run, single-configuration evaluation with no variance, no significance, and no version pinning.** LLM sampling is nondeterministic; the flakiness study needed 30 repetitions to characterize a phenomenon that most papers would have collapsed into one pass/fail number, and it explicitly concedes its own statistical limits ("given the small number of flaky tests in our study, the statistical significance of our results is limited"). Significance testing and effect sizes are close to absent from the test-generation papers retrieved here.

5. **Repair that manufactures passing tests instead of correct oracles.** The evidence is internal to the systems: ChatTester and TestART both fix failures by rewriting assertions; TestART's T3 template substitutes the *observed* value for the expected value in `assertEquals`; A3Test and TestART both report large pass-rate gains. A pipeline that repairs an assertion to match whatever the program currently does will raise pass rate and coverage while *destroying* the oracle — and by the field's own metric convention, that improvement is invisible. Compounding this: generated tests already show **63%** of flakiness from unordered-collection assumptions and systematic **Assertion Roulette**, so "passing" is a weak certificate on multiple independent grounds.
